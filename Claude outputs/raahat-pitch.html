The 3-minute pitch

Two speakers works well: one tells the story, one drives the demo. The green notes are stage cues, not lines.

0:00Hook

Namaste. In 2023, India recorded 57,789 crimes against Scheduled Castes and 12,960 against Scheduled Tribes. That is about 194 cases every single day.

Behind each case is a person who has to file a complaint, sit through an investigation, and face the accused in court, often for years. The fear comes back at every hearing, every threatening call, every postponed date. Most of it is never said out loud, and support arrives only after a crisis.

Cue: landing page on screen, stats strip visible. Pause after "every single day".
0:35What it is

Raahat, which means relief, is an early-warning and support system for survivors and complainants of atrocities. It checks in gently, notices distress early, and brings a trained counsellor in before it becomes a crisis.

It has three views: a private space for the survivor, a prioritised caseload for the counsellor, and district and state oversight for administrators.

1:00The core idea

The heart of Raahat is two layers of detection. The first is machine learning: check-in answers and chat text become one distress score from 0 to 100, with the reasons shown. The second is a fixed safety net in English, Hindi and Hinglish. If someone writes about self-harm, violence, threats or abuse, the rules mark it Critical immediately, whatever the model thinks.

We did this on purpose. In a safety system, a probabilistic model should never be the only line of defence.

Cue: demo driver types I don't want to live anymore. into "See how Raahat listens". The model reads it as Unsure; the safety router still returns Critical 90. Point at both.
1:40Reach

Not everyone has a smartphone. Raahat works on the web, by voice note in their own language, and by a plain phone call: press 1 for a check-in, 9 for emergency. Every path goes into the same risk engine and the same alerts.

2:00Human in the loop

When risk rises, the right counsellor gets a live alert. If nobody acknowledges it within five minutes, it escalates again. The counsellor sees exactly why the score moved, and can override it with a logged reason. Raahat supports human judgement. It never replaces it.

Cue: switch to the counsellor view; show the alert arrive and the explanation panel.
2:25Trust

Monitoring only starts with consent and can be withdrawn at any time. Survivors can download or ask to delete their data. Every staff action is audit-logged. Voice notes are transcribed on our own server.

We are honest about limits: the model is trained on synthetic data, because real survivor data must never be used casually. A pilot needs consented data, clinical validation and a security review, and the architecture is built for that.

2:50Close

Raahat helps the right person notice the right case at the right time. Detect early, support better. Thank you.

Be ready

Judge questions and answers

Answer in two or three sentences, then stop. If they want more, they will ask. Questions tagged "tough" are the ones most likely to trip you up.

Problem and impact

What problem are you solving?

Survivors and complainants of atrocities go through years of investigation and trial. Their distress rises at specific moments: threats, hearings, delays. Today nobody notices until it becomes a crisis. Raahat checks in continuously and gets a counsellor involved early.

Who exactly is the user, and who buys it?

The survivor uses it; counsellors and district officers act on it. The natural owner is the Ministry of Social Justice & Empowerment and state social-welfare departments, through the existing support structures under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, district legal services and NGOs that already counsel survivors.

Why can't counsellors just call everyone regularly?

They can, but one counsellor carries many cases. Raahat does the noticing in between calls and puts the person who needs help today at the top of the list. It turns "check everyone" into "respond to who needs you now".

How will you measure whether it works?

In a pilot: time from a distress signal to first human contact, the share of crises that were flagged before they happened, missed check-ins followed up, and survivor-reported trust and comfort. The most important model metric is false negatives, meaning people in danger whom we missed.

What is new here compared with existing helplines like Tele-MANAS?

Helplines wait for the person to call. Raahat reaches out, follows the person across the whole case, and connects to their own assigned counsellor. We do not replace helplines; we show and link Tele-MANAS 14416 and the atrocity helpline 14566 everywhere.

AI and the model

How does the AI decide someone is distressed?

Two inputs: structured check-in answers (mood, sleep, safety, anxiety, case pressure) and free text from chat or voice. A TF-IDF and logistic-regression model reads the text; a random forest combines everything with missed check-ins and engagement into a 0–100 score and an escalation probability.

Why not deep learning or an LLM for scoring?

Small models are explainable, fast on a basic server, and behave predictably. For each score we show which factors moved it. An LLM is used only to word ordinary, non-urgent chat replies, never to decide whether something is an emergency.

Your metrics say 100% text accuracy. Isn't that suspicious? tough

Yes, and we say so. That is cross-validation on our own synthetic sentences, so it mostly shows the model learned our templates. It is not a real-world accuracy claim. That is exactly why the safety router exists: on real phrasing the model can be unsure, and the rules still catch the emergency.

Don't say "our model is 100% accurate". Say "on synthetic data, which tells us little; validation needs real consented data".

Where did your training data come from?

We generated 1,800 synthetic cases in English, Hindi and Hinglish. Using real survivor data in a hackathon would be unethical. A pilot would retrain on consented, anonymised data with clinical labelling.

What if the model misses someone in danger? tough
  • The safety router checks every message independently and overrides the model for self-harm, violence, threats, abuse, medical emergencies and child safety.
  • An explicit risk-word floor stops a message the model cannot read from lowering the score.
  • A counsellor always sees the case and can override the score.

Live example: "I don't want to live anymore" reads as Unsure to the model, and the router still returns Critical.

How do you avoid false alarms, like "I am not suicidal"?

The router checks for negation within the same clause. "I am not suicidal, just stressed" does not trigger. "I am not suicidal, but I want to die" does, because the second clause is a real risk statement. These cases are in our automated tests.

Why does the demo score stay around 51 for many sentences? tough

The score is built mostly from check-in answers and history. A single sentence with no context lands near the middle. What moves sharply on text alone is the escalation probability and the safety router, which is what the demo highlights. In the real app, a survivor's history moves the score.

Can you predict distress before it happens?

We show a short-term trend forecast on the counsellor's case view, fitted to that person's recent scores, and it shows its uncertainty. It is a nudge to look, not a prediction of the future.

Is the AI biased against any group?

With synthetic data we cannot measure that honestly yet. In a pilot we would report error rates by language, gender and region, prioritise false negatives, and keep a person reviewing every High or Critical case.

Safety and escalation

What happens the moment someone types an emergency?

The case is set to Critical, the survivor sees emergency guidance with the helpline numbers and a calm space, counsellors get a live WebSocket alert, and an external webhook can page on-call staff. If nobody acknowledges within five minutes, it is re-notified.

Does Raahat call the police or an ambulance?

No, and it says so honestly in the chat. It cannot dispatch anyone. It shows 112 and the helplines, and alerts the human care team, who decide what to do.

Can the chatbot say something harmful?

Urgent messages never reach the LLM; they get fixed, reviewed safety replies. Ordinary replies are filtered for false claims such as "I have contacted someone" or promises of confidentiality, and fall back to local replies if Gemini fails or returns something unsafe or cut off.

What is the SOS button for?

One tap marks the case Critical and alerts the care team, with no model involved. On a phone call, pressing 9 does the same. The SOS rate limit is deliberately loose and fails open, so infrastructure problems never block an emergency.

Privacy, consent and ethics

This is very sensitive data. How do you protect it?
  • Role-based access enforced on the server: a survivor can only ever see their own case.
  • Passwords as salted scrypt hashes; session tokens stored as keyed hashes.
  • Every staff view, override and note is audit-logged.
  • A strict Content-Security-Policy, rate limits and single-use WebSocket tickets.
  • Optional automatic purge of old data after a retention period.
Is the survivor being watched without knowing?

No. Monitoring is consent-based and can be withdrawn in settings at any time. The survivor can also download their data or request deletion.

Where does voice audio go?

Voice notes are transcribed on our own server with Whisper. Only if that is unavailable does it fall back to Gemini, and the survivor is told this before recording. Audio is not stored; only the transcript is saved in the chat.

Could the app put a survivor at risk if the accused sees their phone? tough

We thought about this. Read-aloud is off by default so replies are never spoken unexpectedly, and the app gives guidance rather than storing anything on the device beyond the session. Next steps for a pilot would be a quick-exit button and a disguised app icon, common in domestic-violence apps.

Why is the calm space built on Bhagavad Gita quotes? tough

Prepare this one. Many atrocity survivors are Buddhist, Ambedkarite, Christian, Muslim or not religious, and some may find Hindu scripture alienating. A safe answer: "It is one tab among several, and we plan to let survivors choose traditions, including Ambedkar's and Buddhist texts, or secular words of comfort." Better still, make it neutral before the finals.

Access, language and inclusion

What about people without smartphones or internet?

They can call in. A Twilio number or a self-hosted FreeSWITCH line reads the same check-in questions; keypad answers go into the same risk engine. The caller's number is matched to their registered case, and unknown numbers are refused rather than guessed.

Which languages do you support?

English, Hindi and Hinglish in the safety rules, chat and voice. The landing and login pages switch fully to Hindi. Wider coverage (Marathi, Tamil, Bengali and others) is the clearest next step, and the safety rules are designed to be extended per language.

What about people who can't read well?

Voice notes in, read-aloud replies out, phone-call check-ins, large tap targets with faces for moods, and a reduced-motion mode for people who find animation stressful.

What is the court rehearsal feature?

A safe practice space for giving evidence: realistic questions, answered aloud or typed, with an optional pulse reading from the phone camera computed on the device. If stress rises it pauses for grounding. Typed answers are screened by the safety router, so a disclosure during practice still alerts the team.

Don't call the pulse reading medical. It is a wellness estimate.

Technology and scale

What is the tech stack?

React with Tailwind, prebuilt with esbuild; FastAPI on Python; MySQL with Alembic migrations; Redis for shared rate limits and alert fan-out; scikit-learn models; faster-whisper for speech; WebSockets for live alerts; Docker and Gunicorn for deployment; Twilio or FreeSWITCH for phone calls.

Can it scale to a whole state?

The app is stateless behind a load balancer; Redis keeps rate limits and alerts consistent across workers, and the models are small enough to run on ordinary CPUs. Voice transcription is the heaviest part and can move to its own worker pool.

What if Gemini or the internet goes down?

Safety detection and scoring run entirely on our server. A circuit breaker stops calling a failing provider, and chat falls back to written safe replies instantly instead of timing out.

How do you know it actually works, not just a mock-up?

More than 130 automated checks: API access-control and validation tests, production-readiness tests, chatbot safety cases including Hindi and negation, voice tests, and 22 end-to-end browser checks that log in as each role and complete real flows.

What did it cost to build, and to run?

Open-source components throughout. Running cost is mainly a small server and the phone line; Gemini is optional. Self-hosting speech and models keeps per-user cost low and keeps data in the country.

Limitations

What are the limitations? tough

Say these before they ask:

  • The model is trained on synthetic data; real accuracy is unproven.
  • District and state totals on the admin map are sample data.
  • Accounts are demo accounts; there is no self-sign-up or password reset yet.
  • Three languages, not India's full range.
  • The voice-tone signals are experimental and do not affect the score.
  • It is decision support, not a certified clinical or emergency service.
Is it ready to deploy tomorrow?

Not with real survivors tomorrow. The architecture is production-shaped, with Docker, migrations, monitoring and tests. Before a pilot it needs consented data, clinical validation, a security audit and training for counsellors.

What would you build next with three more months?

A district pilot with one NGO; real data and model validation; more Indian languages; quick-exit and disguised-app safety features; and connecting the admin view to real case data.

Rapid-fire

What does "Raahat" mean?

Relief, or comfort, in Hindi and Urdu.

Who is Saathi?

The companion on the landing page. It reacts to the mood a visitor picks, so the first thing a survivor meets feels caring rather than official.

One sentence: why should we pick you?

Raahat is a working system that pairs explainable AI with a safety net that does not depend on it, and it reaches survivors on the web, by voice and by phone.

What was the hardest bug you fixed?

Hindi matching. Standard word boundaries fail on Devanagari vowel signs, so Hindi danger phrases were silently missed. We replaced them with whitespace-based boundaries and added Hindi test cases.
